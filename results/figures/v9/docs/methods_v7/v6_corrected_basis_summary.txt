  A NOTE ON THE GATE ITSELF. `same_model_runs` first rejected all three replicates and left a
  family of one, silently disabling the floor. The cause was not a model difference: `mip_focus`
  was added to the provenance stamp mid-campaign, so the run solved before it carries None while
  the runs solved after carry 0 -- and 0 is what None means, because the stamp reads an
  environment variable that was unset. An absent field is not a difference when the default is
  known; the comparison now normalises it. This is exactly the failure mode the gate exists to
  prevent, in the opposite direction, and it is worth stating that a too-strict gate is as
  dangerous as a missing one: it would have removed the only null the study has.

  At 2030 the non-negative-reduction constraint binds in every run: residual emissions are
  5392.19 Mt against a baseline of 5392.20, identical to within 0.003 Mt across five runs
  spanning 90 to 355 GW of wet-to-dry conversion. Converting a condenser costs 1.5 points of
  thermal efficiency, which raises emissions above baseline; the model buys exactly enough
  biomass co-firing to bring them back.

  The purchase is LINEAR in the conversion: 1.769 PJ of biomass per GW converted
  (R2 = 0.9997 over five runs and three hydrology members). Reserving the non-power share
  therefore obligates roughly 395 PJ/yr of additional biomass at 2030 -- a claim on a
  resource the model does not charge water for, which is the largest single gap between what
  this mechanism costs in the model and what it would cost in the world.

  READ IT AS A MECHANISM, NOT A PRICE. Reserving the non-power share does not simply make the
  system more expensive by 1.34%. It moves money between five terms of comparable size:

    it SPENDS on   dry-cooling O&M            +1.694 pp   (the largest single term)
                   biomass co-firing          +0.992 pp
                   backpressure fuel          +0.819 pp
                   air-retrofit capex         +0.384 pp
    and SAVES on   carbon                     -1.439 pp   (the extra biomass abates)
                   water tariff               -0.980 pp   (dry cooling draws less)
                   coal displaced by biomass  -0.786 pp

  Two things follow that the single headline number hides.

  FIRST, the capex the study is most exposed on is nearly irrelevant to the cost.
  `air_retrofit_capex_cny_per_kw = 300` is an unsourced placeholder against a literature anchor
  near 1370. But air-retrofit capex is only +0.384 pp of the +1.340%. Even a 4.6x increase moves
  the headline to roughly +2.7%, not to a different conclusion -- and the physical response
  (GW converted) was already shown flat across that swing. The exposure is real but bounded, and
  it is bounded by this table rather than by assertion.

  SECOND, the big-M is no longer the story. The unserved-water slack penalty contributes
  +0.074 pp of the +1.340%, i.e. 5.5% of the effect; measured on the volumes in slack_detail.csv
  alone it is +0.041 pp, 3.1%. On the superseded dry-season basis the same term carried roughly
  two thirds of a +5.827% effect. The aggregation correction did not merely shrink the headline
  -- it converted it from a penalty artefact into real spending. Those are the same change:
  a looser right-hand side is one the fleet can satisfy by converting condensers instead of by
  buying its way out at 1000 CNY/m3.

  WHAT IS STILL UNSATISFACTORY. `slack_penalty_cny_per_unit` has never been perturbed, and the
  water slack is priced by a bare literal 1000.0 at solver.py:711 rather than by that field. At
  3-6% of the effect this no longer threatens the headline, but the price of infeasibility should
  be a swept parameter, not a constant nobody has varied.

  So the model may install dry cooling, pay for it, and then operate it WET -- and nothing in the
  outputs records the stock. Reconstructing a lower bound on the stock as each plant's running-max
  operating fraction, applied to that year's surviving capacity (so retirement cannot masquerade

  WA_cwatm_126_dry
      year  operated GW   stock GW  run wet GW  % of stock
      2030        127.1      127.1         0.0          0%

      2050         49.6      100.8        51.3         51%
      2060         13.4       47.0        33.6         71%

  THE DIVERGENCE IS LARGE AND IT CHANGES WHAT FIG 5 SAYS. In the binding run 97 GW -- 69% of the
  surviving dry-cooling stock -- is being operated WET at 2060, and 47 GW (21%) at 2050. So the
  headline series "conversion peaks at 380 GW and falls to 44 GW" is an OPERATING share. The stock
  falls only to 141 GW. Fig 5's annotation attributes the whole decline to "converted stock
  retiring, not a policy reversal"; that is right for part of it and wrong for the rest, because
  the remainder is capacity reverting to wet operation once water stops binding at its node.

  Economically the behaviour is correct -- running dry costs 1.5 points of efficiency in fuel and
  CO2, so once the basin constraint slackens (the northern fleet has largely retired by 2060) wet
  operation is cheaper. It is a genuine and interesting result: the fleet does not stay converted,
  it converts for the transition and reverts afterwards. But it must be SAID, and it cannot be
  verified from the outputs because `air_installed` is never written.

  ACTIONS: (1) emit `air_installed` from the results writer; (2) relabel the Fig 5 annotation to
  separate retirement from reversion; (3) report the stock series alongside the operated series.
